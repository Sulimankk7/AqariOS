# MODULE 6 — RENT PAYMENTS & CHEQUE HANDLING

Continuation of `PropertyOS_Phase3_Physical_Design.md`, `PropertyOS_Module4_Properties.md`, and `PropertyOS_Module5_Leasing.md`. Applies the same Global Conventions (UUIDv7 PKs, `created_at`/`updated_at`/`created_by`/`updated_by`, soft delete, `company_id` + RLS tenant isolation, `snake_case` naming) unless a deviation is explicitly justified.

Scale target for this module: **2,000,000+ rent payment records, 500,000+ cheque records (a meaningful fraction of payments are cheque-settled, per Jordanian market practice), 10,000+ companies.**

## 6.0 Module-Wide Design Decisions (read before the tables)

**Why three tables, not two.** The brief names `rent_payments` and `cheque_details` explicitly, but a careful read of the "Full Payment / Partial Payment / Overpayment / Outstanding Balance" requirement set surfaces a real modeling gap that a naive two-table design cannot correctly represent: **a single `rent_payments` row cannot, by itself, express partial payment against a billing obligation, nor can it express one payment covering multiple obligations** (e.g., a tenant paying two months of back-rent in a single bank transfer, or a single large payment that partially covers the current period and leaves the remainder as a credit toward next period). A model where `rent_payments` is *only* "one row per billing period, mutated as money arrives" cannot cleanly answer "which specific payment(s) settled this specific obligation" once overpayment/underpayment/multi-period settlement enters the picture, and mutating a payment row's `amount_paid` upward every time more money arrives destroys the audit trail of *when* each increment was actually received.

The resolution — consistent with the separation-of-concerns principle used everywhere else in this schema (`subscription_plans`/`company_subscriptions`, `lease_contracts`/`contract_terminations`) — is:

- **`rent_payments`** represents **the billing obligation and the receipt-of-funds events against it, unified**: each row is one scheduled (or ad-hoc) rent installment — "Month of March 2026 rent, apartment X, contract Y, due 2026-03-01, amount 350.000 JOD." Its `status`/`amount_paid`/`paid_date` columns reflect the *current cumulative settlement state* of that specific obligation, updated transactionally as allocations are applied against it.
- **`payment_allocations`** is the table the brief's "Invalid payment allocations / Over-allocation" integrity requirements are actually about: it is the many-to-many linking layer between **a physical receipt-of-funds event** and **the specific `rent_payments` obligation(s) that money was applied to**, carrying the allocated amount as its own business-meaningful attribute — the exact "M:M relationship deserves to be a first-class entity" pattern already established platform-wide (Phase 2 §2.1, note on M:M relationships). This is what makes overpayment (one payment, amount left unallocated / carried forward), underpayment (one obligation, multiple partial allocations from multiple payment events over time), and multi-period settlement (one payment event, allocated across several `rent_payments` rows) all representable without ever mutating history in place.
- **`cheque_details`** remains the 1:1 extension of a *physical payment event* specifically when that event's method is `cheque` — its own lifecycle (issued → received → deposited → cleared/bounced/cancelled) is independent of, and often trails, the underlying rent obligation's own status, which is precisely why it cannot be collapsed into `rent_payments` itself (a bounced cheque must retroactively *reverse* an allocation that already happened, which the allocation-layer indirection makes clean).

**Payment event vs. billing obligation — the conceptual split made concrete.** Rather than introduce a fourth table purely to hold "the physical receipt-of-funds event" as a distinct concept from "the billing obligation it settles" (which would deviate further from the brief's explicit two-named-tables-plus-allocations framing), this design keeps `rent_payments` doing double duty **only for the common case** (one payment event settling exactly one obligation in full, on time — the overwhelming majority of Jordanian residential rent collection in practice) while `payment_allocations` is the explicit, mandatory mechanism for every case that isn't that simple shape. Concretely:

- A **scheduled installment** is created as a `rent_payments` row by the monthly billing job (per Phase 1 §1.16) with `status = 'pending'`, `amount_paid = 0`, `paid_date = NULL`.
- When money actually arrives, the application creates (or reuses) a **settlement record** — still a `rent_payments`-shaped row when it's a simple 1:1 full settlement, but the actual *linkage* between "money received" and "obligation settled" always flows through `payment_allocations`, even in the simple case, so that the exact same code path handles simple and complex settlement uniformly rather than branching between "just update the row" and "create allocations" depending on payment shape. This uniformity is deliberate: **every rent collection, without exception, produces at least one `payment_allocations` row**, which is what allows `payment_allocations` to be the single source of truth for "how much of obligation X has actually been settled, and by which specific payment(s)" — the `rent_payments.amount_paid`/`status` columns become denormalized, trigger-maintained caches of the sum of their allocations, exactly mirroring the `apartments.occupancy_status` cached-derived-column pattern already established in Module 4.
- A **standalone payment event that isn't yet allocated to any specific obligation** (money received via bank transfer with an ambiguous reference, requiring manual reconciliation before staff assigns it to a specific month) is represented as a `rent_payments` row with `payment_purpose = 'unallocated_receipt'` and `lease_contract_id`/obligation linkage still present (a payment must always belong to a contract, per the stated requirement, even before it's been allocated to a specific billing period) but zero `payment_allocations` rows yet — staff later create the allocation(s) once reconciled, at which point the receiving row's own `status` is `settled_fully_unapplied` until allocated.

This keeps exactly two tables doing "payment work" (`rent_payments` for both scheduled obligations and physical receipt events, `payment_allocations` for the linkage), matching the brief's three named tables while still correctly solving the partial/overpayment/multi-period requirements that a naive reading would otherwise miss.

**Denormalized `company_id`, `lease_contract_id`, `tenant_id` on every table in this module.** Consistent with the module-wide denormalization principle already established in Module 4 §4.0 and Module 5 §5.0: every table here carries `company_id` directly, and `rent_payments`/`payment_allocations` additionally carry `tenant_id` denormalized from `lease_contracts.tenant_id`, because "tenant payment history" (a named required query shape) must not require a join through `lease_contracts` on what will be this schema's single highest-write-volume table family. The cost is a handful of denormalized UUID columns kept in sync at INSERT time from the parent contract, which never changes after the row is written (a payment's tenant/contract association is immutable — a payment is never administratively reassigned to a different tenant).

**Why `rent_payments` is this module's partitioning candidate, flagged since Module 5 §5.7.** At 2,000,000+ rows and growing without bound (every active lease generates a new row every billing cycle, forever), this table has exactly the shape that has justified RANGE partitioning everywhere else in this schema (`meter_readings`, `audit_logs`, `login_history`): append-heavy, naturally time-ordered via `due_date`, and every stated query pattern (payment history, outstanding balances, late payments, dashboards) is bounded to a specific contract/tenant/company or a recent/bounded date range rather than needing a full-table scan. Full treatment in §6.4, including the treatment of rows whose `due_date` is legitimately `NULL` (§6.0 below and §6.4).

**`due_date` is a business fact — an obligation's actual due date — not a partitioning-routing value.** `rent_payments.due_date` represents when a real payment obligation is contractually due. It is populated for every `payment_purpose = 'scheduled_installment'` row (the obligation's due date is the entire point of that row). It is **legitimately `NULL`** for `payment_purpose = 'unallocated_receipt'` rows (an unreconciled receipt has no due date by definition) and for the majority of `payment_purpose = 'adjustment'` rows (most ad-hoc adjustments — a negotiated one-off credit, for instance — do not represent a collectible obligation with its own due date). An `adjustment` row carries a populated `due_date` only in the specific case where the adjustment itself represents a real, collectible obligation with a genuine due date (e.g., a one-off negotiated charge the tenant must pay by a specific date). `due_date` is never populated with a synthetic value (such as `created_at::date`) merely to give a row a non-NULL value — doing so would misrepresent an unreconciled receipt or a dateless adjustment as having a real contractual due date it does not have. See §6.4 for how this interacts with this table's RANGE partitioning strategy.

**Cheque lifecycle as an explicit state machine, not a free-text status.** Per the stated requirement, `cheque_details.status` is a proper enum (`issued`, `received`, `deposited`, `cleared`, `bounced`, `cancelled`) with date columns whose population is gated by which states have been reached — a cheque cannot have a `clearance_date` without first having a `deposit_date`, cannot be `bounced` without a `bounce_reason`, and so on. This mirrors the `company_subscriptions`/`lease_contracts` philosophy of "the business genuinely needs to distinguish *why*/*at what stage* something is, not just a boolean" — a bounced cheque and a cancelled cheque are both "did not result in cleared funds," but they have entirely different operational and legal consequences (a bounced cheque is often itself a criminal matter under Jordanian law — إصدار شيك بدون رصيد — and drives a distinct collections workflow; a cancelled cheque is typically a mutually-agreed non-event). Cheque-level bounce state lives exclusively on `cheque_details.status = 'bounced'` — it is **not** duplicated onto `rent_payments.due_date_status` (see §6.1's enum definition, which does not include a `bounced` value).

## 6.1 Table: `rent_payments`

**Purpose:** Represents both (a) a scheduled rent billing obligation for a specific lease contract and billing period, and (b) — once settled — the aggregate record of funds received against that obligation, with the authoritative settlement detail living in the child `payment_allocations` table. This is the immutable ledger row Phase 1 §1.16 designates as the financial record of rent collection.

**Business Description:** One row per billing-period obligation under a lease (monthly/quarterly/semi-annual/annual, per `lease_contracts.payment_frequency`), generated on a schedule by the billing job per Phase 1 §1.16, though ad-hoc manual creation remains supported for adjustments, backdated entries, and the "unallocated receipt awaiting reconciliation" case described in §6.0. Captures the due amount, the cumulative amount actually settled (a trigger-maintained cache of `payment_allocations`), payment method, references, and status across the full pending → paid / partially_paid / late / overdue lifecycle.

**Primary Key:** `id`

**Foreign Keys:**
- `company_id` → `companies(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT.
- `lease_contract_id` → `lease_contracts(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT — per Phase 2, the payment ledger is immutable financial record; a contract can never be hard-deleted while payment history references it.
- `tenant_id` → `tenants(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT.
- `building_id` → `buildings(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT.
- `apartment_id` → `apartments(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT.
- `created_by`, `updated_by`, `deleted_by` → `users(id)` ON DELETE SET NULL.

**Unique Constraints:**
- `uq_rent_payments_contract_period` on `(lease_contract_id, billing_period_start, billing_period_end)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL AND payment_purpose = 'scheduled_installment'` — prevents the billing job (or a careless manual entry) from generating two competing obligations for the exact same contract and period; deliberately scoped to `payment_purpose = 'scheduled_installment'` only, since `unallocated_receipt`/`adjustment` rows are not period-bound in the same sense and legitimately may share overlapping "period" values (or have none meaningfully set) without representing a duplicate-billing error.

**Check Constraints:**
- `chk_rent_payments_period_dates` — `billing_period_end IS NULL OR billing_period_start IS NULL OR billing_period_end > billing_period_start`.
- `chk_rent_payments_amount_due_nonneg` — `amount_due >= 0` — zero is valid for an `adjustment` row representing a pure credit with no charge component; negative is never valid (a credit is expressed via a negative `payment_allocations.allocated_amount` against a zero/positive `amount_due` row instead, keeping the sign convention consistent and centralized in the allocation layer rather than split across two tables' worth of sign logic).
- `chk_rent_payments_amount_paid_nonneg` — `amount_paid >= 0` — the cached aggregate can never go negative regardless of how allocations/reversals net out; enforced here as a backstop even though the maintaining trigger should already guarantee it, per the "defense in depth, don't rely solely on trigger correctness" posture already applied to `lease_contracts` immutability (Module 5 §5.1).
- `chk_rent_payments_scheduled_period_purpose` — the direct implementation of this table's period/due-date nullability rule, restored to its original two-sided business-accurate form:
  - `(payment_purpose = 'scheduled_installment') → (billing_period_start IS NOT NULL AND billing_period_end IS NOT NULL AND due_date IS NOT NULL)` — a genuine scheduled installment must carry real period and due-date data.
  - `(payment_purpose IN ('unallocated_receipt', 'adjustment')) → (billing_period_start IS NULL AND billing_period_end IS NULL)` — a non-scheduled row is not period-bound.
  - `due_date` itself remains nullable at the column level for non-scheduled rows and is **not** governed by this constraint beyond the `scheduled_installment` branch above — an `unallocated_receipt` row has `due_date IS NULL` by definition, and an `adjustment` row's `due_date` is populated only when that specific adjustment represents a real collectible obligation with a genuine due date (an application-layer judgment call, not a DB-enforced one, since the database cannot distinguish "this credit adjustment has no due date" from "this charge adjustment has a real one" without a further discriminating column this brief does not call for).

**Indexes:**
- `idx_rent_payments_contract_due_date` composite on `(lease_contract_id, due_date DESC)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — **the** "Contract Payment History" query (stated requirement): full payment ledger for one contract, most-recent-first. `lease_contract_id` leads as the mandatory equality filter (every contract-detail-page payment tab hits this shape); `due_date DESC` trails so the common "most recent installments first" access is a direct ordered index scan with zero separate sort step, mirroring the identical composite-with-trailing-DESC pattern established throughout Modules 3–5. Rows with `due_date IS NULL` sort last under standard PostgreSQL `DESC` NULL-ordering and remain fully queryable via this index; they are simply not the common case this index's ordering optimizes for.
  - *Queries served:* `SELECT * FROM rent_payments WHERE lease_contract_id = $1 AND deleted_at IS NULL ORDER BY due_date DESC LIMIT 50` (contract detail page, paginated).
  - *Expected improvement:* turns a filter-then-sort over a table headed toward millions of rows into a single ordered index range-scan bounded by one contract's row count (typically dozens over a contract's life) — effectively O(log n + k) instead of O(n log n).
  - *Why preferred over separate `lease_contract_id` and `due_date` indexes:* a composite serves both the equality filter and the sort order in one structure; two separate single-column indexes would require PostgreSQL to pick one, bitmap-AND against the other, and still sort the result — strictly worse for this exact, extremely common access shape.
- `idx_rent_payments_tenant_due_date` composite on `(tenant_id, due_date DESC)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — **the** "Tenant Payment History" query (stated requirement): a tenant's complete payment record *across all their contracts* (a returning tenant with multiple historical leases, or a tenant portal's "my payment history" view), which the contract-scoped index above cannot serve without a multi-contract union.
  - *Queries served:* tenant portal payment history; leasing-agent reliability check across a prospective returning tenant's full history before approving a new lease.
  - *Expected improvement:* identical class of improvement as the contract-scoped index above, but for the tenant-as-aggregation-root access pattern instead of contract-as-root.
  - *Why preferred:* without this index, "this tenant's full history" would require either a join through `lease_contracts` (extra hop, extra index requirement on that table) or a sequential scan; the denormalized `tenant_id` (§6.0) plus this dedicated index removes both costs.
- `idx_rent_payments_company_status_due_date` composite on `(company_id, due_date_status, due_date)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — the primary **Dashboard Statistics** and **Outstanding Balances** query shape: "all currently-late/overdue payments for company X, ordered by how overdue" and "collections aging" style reports. `company_id` leads (mandatory tenant scope, present in every query against this table), `due_date_status` second as the near-universal secondary equality filter (dashboards ask for one specific status bucket — `late`, `overdue_unpaid` — at a time), `due_date` trailing to support the aging/ordering dimension within that status bucket.
  - *Queries served:* `SELECT * FROM rent_payments WHERE company_id = $1 AND due_date_status IN ('late','overdue_unpaid') AND deleted_at IS NULL ORDER BY due_date ASC`; "collections aging" report bucketing by days-overdue; the KPI tile "X JOD outstanding this month."
  - *Expected improvement:* without this index, a company-wide "what's overdue right now" query — evaluated on essentially every owner/manager dashboard load — would sequential-scan a table headed toward millions of rows; with it, it's a targeted index range-scan bounded to the (typically small, relative to total history) set of currently-problematic rows.
  - *Why this ordering:* `company_id` must lead for RLS/tenant-scoping alignment (every query is tenant-scoped, none are cross-tenant); `due_date_status` before `due_date` because status is the coarser, near-always-present equality filter, while `due_date` is a range/sort refinement layered on top — the standard equality-before-range column ordering convention applied consistently since Module 3.
- `idx_rent_payments_late_overdue` composite on `(company_id, due_date)` WHERE `due_date_status IN ('late', 'overdue_unpaid') AND deleted_at IS NULL` — a **deliberately narrower, partial-on-status** companion to the index above, purpose-built for the single highest-frequency dashboard query ("show me only what's late/overdue, nothing else") as opposed to the broader status-inclusive index which also serves less-common status-specific queries. This mirrors the exact `idx_apartments_company_occupancy_bedrooms`-style "narrowest, most surgical partial index reserved for the single highest-value query" pattern from Module 4 §4.4.
  - *Queries served:* the "Late Payments" stated requirement's core dashboard widget and the automated overdue-reminder notification job's daily sweep (Module 8, forward reference).
  - *Expected improvement:* because this index only ever contains currently-problematic rows (a small fraction of total payment history at any given time — most payments do eventually settle and move out of this partial index's row set), it stays small and fast indefinitely regardless of how large the overall `rent_payments` table grows, unlike the broader composite above which — while still efficient — carries more dead weight from historical `paid`/`cancelled` rows it must still be capable of serving.
- `idx_rent_payments_apartment_due_date` composite on `(apartment_id, due_date DESC)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — supports "payment history for this specific unit across all tenancies" (a unit-level financial history view spanning multiple tenants over the unit's life, distinct from a single contract's history) — a real owner-reporting need ("has this unit historically been a reliable earner") that neither the contract-scoped nor tenant-scoped index above can serve without a multi-row union.
  - *Queries served:* apartment/unit detail page's financial-history tab, spanning all historical tenancies.
  - *Expected improvement:* same class of benefit as the contract/tenant composites, for the unit-as-root access pattern.
- `idx_rent_payments_receipt_number` on `(receipt_number)` WHERE `receipt_number IS NOT NULL AND deleted_at IS NULL` — supports the stated **Search** requirement: staff looking up a payment by its printed receipt number (e.g., resolving a tenant dispute where they present a paper receipt). Not a composite with `company_id` because `receipt_number` values are already effectively unique platform-wide in practice (per-company gapless sequences per Phase 1 §1.20's `company_receipt_sequences` rarely if ever collide across companies in the same value range at any given time, but even if they did, this index still narrows to a tiny candidate set that a subsequent `company_id` filter resolves trivially).
- `idx_rent_payments_payment_reference_trgm` — GIN trigram index on `payment_reference_number` — supports the stated **Search** requirement for staff searching by partial bank-transfer reference (bank references are often long alphanumeric strings a staff member recalls only a fragment of) — same rationale class as `idx_lease_contracts_contract_number_trgm` (Module 5) and `idx_buildings_name_trgm` (Module 4).

**Deliberately omitted index:** no standalone `payment_method` index. Filtering/reporting by payment method ("how much did we collect via cheque this month") is a real but low-frequency analytical query, adequately served by scanning within the already-narrow `idx_rent_payments_company_status_due_date` result set or via `report_snapshots` (Module 1 §1.33) rather than a dedicated hot-path index — consistent with the "avoid unnecessary indexes" discipline applied without exception since Module 3.

**Business Rules:**
- `amount_paid` and `due_date_status` are **both cached/derived columns**, never written directly outside their maintaining trigger — the authoritative settlement facts live in `payment_allocations`. An `AFTER INSERT OR UPDATE OR DELETE ON payment_allocations FOR EACH ROW` trigger recomputes the affected `rent_payments` row's `amount_paid` as `SUM(payment_allocations.allocated_amount WHERE rent_payment_id = ... AND deleted_at IS NULL)`.
- **Grace-period-aware `due_date_status` derivation.** The trigger derives `due_date_status` by comparing `amount_paid` against `amount_due`, and — for rows with a non-NULL `due_date` — comparing `CURRENT_DATE` against `due_date` **offset by the owning company's configured grace period** (`company_settings.rent_grace_period_days`, Module 1 §1.2), rather than against `due_date` directly. This makes the already-captured, already-exposed `rent_grace_period_days` setting actually function: a company that configures a 5-day grace period genuinely sees late/overdue determination shift by 5 days, rather than the setting being silently ignored. Concretely, where `grace_period_days` is looked up from the row's owning company's `company_settings`:
  - `amount_paid = 0` and `CURRENT_DATE <= due_date + grace_period_days` → `pending`.
  - `amount_paid = 0` and `CURRENT_DATE > due_date + grace_period_days` → `overdue_unpaid`.
  - `0 < amount_paid < amount_due` and `CURRENT_DATE <= due_date + grace_period_days` → `partially_paid`.
  - `0 < amount_paid < amount_due` and `CURRENT_DATE > due_date + grace_period_days` → `late` (a partial payment received but the obligation is not yet fully settled past its grace-adjusted due date — distinct from `overdue_unpaid`, which has received nothing at all; this distinction matters for collections triage, since a tenant who has paid *something* is a different risk profile than one who has paid nothing).
  - `amount_paid >= amount_due` → `paid` (an overpayment, per the stated requirement, still resolves this specific obligation to `paid` — the excess is tracked as unapplied credit at the payment-event level via `payment_allocations`, not by this obligation row somehow reflecting a status "beyond paid").
  - **For a row with `due_date IS NULL`** (an `unallocated_receipt` row, or a dateless `adjustment` row): no late/overdue state can be derived, since there is no due date to compare against. `due_date_status` for such a row reflects only the `amount_paid`-vs-`amount_due` comparison — `pending` (nothing applied yet), `partially_paid`, or `paid` — and never resolves to `late` or `overdue_unpaid`, since deriving either would require a due date that does not exist for these rows.
  - A cheque-settled allocation later reversed due to bounce transitions the affected row's recomputed status back down accordingly (e.g., from `paid` back to `pending`/`overdue_unpaid`) — see `cheque_details` §6.2 for the bounce-reversal mechanism. The affected row's own `due_date_status` is recomputed to one of the values in the enum below (never to a distinct "bounced" value on this table — see the next bullet).
  - `cancelled` is set only by explicit application action (an obligation waived/voided), never derived from the amount comparison, and is excluded from the trigger's automatic recomputation once set (an application-layer guard, not a DB one, prevents the trigger from "reviving" a deliberately cancelled row — documented here as a known trigger-scope boundary for Phase 8 implementation).
- **`due_date_status_enum` domain:** `pending | paid | partially_paid | late | overdue_unpaid | cancelled`. There is **no** `bounced` value on this enum. Cheque-bounce state is represented exclusively by `cheque_details.status = 'bounced'` (§6.2); the affected `rent_payments` row's `due_date_status` is recomputed by the bounce-reversal mechanism into one of the five derived values above (typically `pending` or `overdue_unpaid`, depending on the grace-adjusted due date at the time of reversal), never into a separate `bounced` value duplicating cheque-specific state onto the payment-obligation row. This keeps the payment-obligation status and the cheque-instrument status as two distinct, non-duplicative facts, each on its own table.
- `amount_due` is a **snapshot**, immutable after creation except by explicit adjustment-with-audit-trail — it reflects what was actually owed for that specific historical period even if the contract's rent later changes via renewal, mirroring the exact `company_subscriptions.price_at_subscription` / `lease_contracts` (itself, as a snapshot of a prior renewal's terms) historical-integrity pattern used throughout this schema.
- A payment's `lease_contract_id`/`tenant_id`/`building_id`/`apartment_id` are set once at INSERT and never reassigned — consistent with the identical "denormalized-at-write, immutable thereafter" rule already established for `lease_contracts.building_id` in Module 5 §5.1.
- `due_date` is set once at INSERT (or left `NULL` for non-scheduled rows per the nullability rule above) and never independently reassigned, except by an explicit, audited administrative correction (e.g., a genuine data-entry error on a scheduled installment's due date) — it is never backfilled with a synthetic value to satisfy anything other than the row's own real business meaning.

**Soft Delete Strategy:** Standard, but exceptionally rare in practice for the same reason as `lease_contracts` (Module 5 §5.1) — a payment ledger row is a financial record; `deleted_at` is reserved for genuine data-entry errors (a duplicate obligation accidentally generated twice by a billing-job bug), never for reversing a real payment, which is handled by reversing the specific `payment_allocations` row(s) instead (preserving the fact that money was received and later returned/reversed, rather than erasing the event).

**Audit Requirements:** Every mutation logged to `audit_logs`; `amount_due`, `due_date_status` transitions, and any `deleted_at` soft-delete on this table are high-severity events given direct financial/legal weight. Because `amount_paid`/`due_date_status` are trigger-maintained rather than application-written, the audit-logging responsibility for *those specific columns'* changes is carried by auditing the `payment_allocations` mutations that drove the recomputation (§6.3), not by attempting to audit-log a trigger-internal UPDATE separately — avoiding a confusing double-audit-trail for what is really one business event viewed from two tables.

**Future Scalability Notes:** Explicitly named in the scale target (2,000,000+ rows) and the direct successor concern flagged in Module 5 §5.7. Full partitioning treatment in §6.4 below (shared analysis across all three tables in this module, since `cheque_details` and `payment_allocations` scale in lockstep with `rent_payments`), including the explicit treatment of this table's legitimately-nullable `due_date` column under RANGE partitioning.

## 6.2 Table: `cheque_details`

**Purpose:** 1:1 extension of a payment event specifically when `payment_method = 'cheque'`, modeling the post-dated-cheque lifecycle (issued → received → deposited → cleared/bounced/cancelled) that is inseparable from Jordanian rental payment practice per Phase 1 §1.16.

**Business Description:** Post-dated cheques (شيكات آجلة) are a standard Jordanian landlord collection instrument — a tenant issues a batch of cheques at lease signing, one per future billing period, each individually deposited and cleared (or bounced) on its own due date. This table captures that full lifecycle as an explicit state machine with the dates and failure detail the business genuinely needs to track, distinct from a generic `payment_method` enum value on `rent_payments` which could never express "deposited but not yet cleared" as a queryable state.

**Primary Key:** `id`

**Foreign Keys:**
- `company_id` → `companies(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT.
- `rent_payment_id` → `rent_payments(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT — per Phase 2, the post-dated cheque lifecycle is inseparable from its payment row and preserved permanently.
- `lease_contract_id` → `lease_contracts(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT.
- `tenant_id` → `tenants(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT.
- `replacement_cheque_id` → `cheque_details(id)` ON DELETE SET NULL — self-referencing; a replacement cheque record's own independent existence should not be threatened by any future change to the original's row (SET NULL rather than RESTRICT, since unlike `lease_contracts.prior_contract_id`'s renewal-chain semantics, this link is informational cross-reference, not the structural backbone of an immutability guarantee).
- `created_by`, `updated_by`, `deleted_by` → `users(id)` ON DELETE SET NULL.

**Unique Constraints:**
- `uq_cheque_details_rent_payment_id` on `(rent_payment_id)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — enforces the 1:1 cardinality with its parent payment row.
- `uq_cheque_details_company_cheque_number` on `(company_id, cheque_number, bank_name)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — the direct implementation of the stated **"Duplicate cheque numbers (within the same company)"** integrity requirement. Deliberately scoped to `(company_id, cheque_number, bank_name)` rather than `(company_id, cheque_number)` alone: cheque numbers are bank-issued sequences, not globally unique identifiers even within one bank, and in principle two different tenants banking at two different banks could coincidentally share a cheque number — including `bank_name` in the composite avoids a false-positive rejection of two legitimately different physical cheques that happen to share a number at different banks, while still catching the real error case (the same bank+number combination recorded twice for the same company, which would indicate either a duplicate data entry or, more seriously, a potential fraud/reuse scenario worth surfacing rather than silently allowing).

**Check Constraints:**
- `chk_cheque_details_amount_positive` — `amount > 0`.
- `chk_cheque_details_due_after_issue` — `due_date >= issue_date` — a post-dated cheque's payable date cannot precede its issue date; equality is allowed (a cheque issued and immediately payable, i.e., not actually post-dated, is a valid degenerate case, e.g., a same-day cash-equivalent cheque payment).
- `chk_cheque_details_received_after_issue` — `received_date IS NULL OR received_date >= issue_date` — the company cannot take custody of a cheque before it was issued.
- `chk_cheque_details_deposit_requires_received` — `deposit_date IS NULL OR received_date IS NOT NULL` — a cheque cannot be deposited before the company has recorded receiving it; enforces logical lifecycle ordering at the data level.
- `chk_cheque_details_deposit_after_received` — `deposit_date IS NULL OR deposit_date >= received_date` — cannot deposit before receipt.
- `chk_cheque_details_clearance_requires_deposit` — `clearance_date IS NULL OR deposit_date IS NOT NULL` — a cheque cannot clear without first having been deposited; the structural implementation of the stated **"Invalid cheque dates"** integrity requirement's core case.
- `chk_cheque_details_clearance_after_deposit` — `clearance_date IS NULL OR clearance_date >= deposit_date`.
- `chk_cheque_details_bounce_requires_deposit` — `bounce_date IS NULL OR deposit_date IS NOT NULL` — symmetric with clearance: a cheque cannot bounce without first having been deposited (a cheque can be *cancelled* pre-deposit, but "bounced" is specifically a bank-rejection outcome that presupposes a deposit attempt occurred).
- `chk_cheque_details_status_date_consistency` — a comprehensive per-status gate: `(status = 'issued') OR (status = 'received' AND received_date IS NOT NULL) OR (status = 'deposited' AND deposit_date IS NOT NULL) OR (status = 'cleared' AND clearance_date IS NOT NULL) OR (status = 'bounced' AND bounce_date IS NOT NULL AND bounce_reason IS NOT NULL) OR (status = 'cancelled' AND cancellation_reason IS NOT NULL)` — this is the single constraint that ties the enum status to the specific date/reason fields the stated requirements say must be present at each stage, catching the exact class of error ("marked cleared but no clearance_date recorded") that a bare enum column without this correlation check would silently allow.
- `chk_cheque_details_not_both_bounced_and_cleared` — `NOT (status = 'cleared' AND bounce_date IS NOT NULL)` is intentionally **not** imposed as a hard block on `bounce_date` being non-NULL alongside a `cleared` status, because a cheque can legitimately be deposited, bounce once, be re-deposited by the company (a real, common Jordanian banking practice — a first bounce is sometimes a timing issue, and the payee re-presents the same physical cheque), and clear on the second attempt — in that case `bounce_date` correctly remains populated as history while `status` has moved on to `cleared`. Enforcing mutual exclusivity here would reject this legitimate, common re-presentment scenario; the current `status` value alone is what's authoritative for "where does this cheque stand right now," not the mere presence of a historical `bounce_date`.
- `chk_cheque_details_future_dates` — `issue_date <= CURRENT_DATE + INTERVAL '2 years'` — a loose sanity bound (not a strict "cannot be future" rule, since post-dated cheques are *by definition* often issued today for a due date well in the future) guarding only against a plainly-erroneous data-entry date (e.g., a typo'd year decades out); `due_date` itself is deliberately **not** bounded against `CURRENT_DATE` at all, since a legitimately-issued post-dated cheque due many months out is the entire point of this table.

**Indexes:**
- `uq_cheque_details_rent_payment_id` (above) — doubles as the primary "get the cheque record for this payment" lookup from the payment detail page.
- `idx_cheque_details_lease_contract_id` composite on `(lease_contract_id, due_date)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — supports the leasing-agent's "review this tenant's full post-dated cheque batch, in due-date order" workflow at contract level — a real, frequent operational screen (verifying at signing that all 12 monthly cheques were received, then tracking each as its due date approaches).
  - *Queries served:* `SELECT * FROM cheque_details WHERE lease_contract_id = $1 ORDER BY due_date`; the "upcoming cheques to deposit this week" operational worklist, scoped to one contract or unioned across a company (see next index for the company-wide version).
  - *Expected improvement:* index-ordered scan instead of sequential scan + sort, identical class of benefit as the `rent_payments` contract-history index.
- `idx_cheque_details_company_status_due_date` composite on `(company_id, status, due_date)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — the company-wide operational worklist: "which cheques are due for deposit this week across the whole portfolio" (`status = 'received'`, `due_date` in the coming days) and "which cheques have bounced and need collections follow-up" (`status = 'bounced'`) — both are named-adjacent to the stated **Dashboard Statistics** and **Late Payments**-style requirements applied to the cheque lifecycle specifically.
  - *Queries served:* the daily "cheques to deposit today/this week" ops screen; the "bounced cheques requiring follow-up" collections dashboard.
  - *Expected improvement:* targeted index range-scan against what will be a small subset of total historical cheque rows at any given time (most cheques eventually reach a terminal `cleared` state and drop out of active operational relevance), keeping this dashboard fast indefinitely as history accumulates.
- `idx_cheque_details_tenant_id` on `(tenant_id)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — supports a tenant-level cheque reliability check ("has this tenant's cheques historically bounced") — a real underwriting question mirroring `idx_lease_guarantors_national_id`'s rationale in the prior Leasing module design, evaluated before approving a new lease for a returning tenant.
- `idx_cheque_details_cheque_number_trgm` — GIN trigram index on `cheque_number` — supports the stated **Search** requirement for staff locating a cheque by (partial) physical cheque number, e.g. resolving a bank-reconciliation discrepancy.

**Deliberately omitted index:** no standalone `bank_name` index. "All cheques from Bank X" is not a stated or realistic per-request query — bank-level analysis, if ever needed, is a `report_snapshots`-served analytical query, not a hot-path lookup, consistent with the discipline applied to `payment_method` on `rent_payments` above.

**Business Rules:**
- A bounced cheque (`status` transitions to `bounced`) triggers an application-orchestrated (not DB-trigger) reversal: the `payment_allocations` row(s) that were created against this cheque's linked `rent_payment_id` on the assumption of eventual clearance must be reversed (soft-deleted / flagged `reversed`, never hard-deleted, per §6.3's own immutability rule), which in turn causes the `rent_payments.amount_paid`/`due_date_status` trigger to recompute the obligation back down to its pre-payment (or partially-paid, if other allocations remain) state — landing on one of `due_date_status_enum`'s five derived values (`pending`/`overdue_unpaid`/`partially_paid`/`late`/`paid`), never a distinct "bounced" value, per §6.0/§6.1. This is a single multi-table transaction, consistent with how a lease renewal or contract termination is also a single multi-table application-orchestrated transaction elsewhere in this schema (Module 5 §5.0, §5.2).
- `deposit_date`/`clearance_date`/`bounce_date` populate strictly in forward lifecycle order — enforced by the CHECK constraints above at the single-row level; the *sequencing of status transitions themselves* (e.g., disallowing a jump directly from `issued` to `cleared` without passing through `received`/`deposited`) is an application-layer workflow guard, not a DB constraint, since expressing "valid state transition graph" fully in SQL would require a trigger inspecting the row's *previous* value on UPDATE — flagged here as the recommended Phase 8 hardening (a `BEFORE UPDATE` trigger validating `OLD.status → NEW.status` against an allowed-transitions table), mirroring the identical "application-layer today, DB-trigger hardening recommended for Phase 8" posture already used for `lease_contracts` immutability (Module 5 §5.1) and cheque status corresponds date fields (imposed here at the DB level, unlike the full transition graph, because the *date-consistency* rule is expressible as a stateless single-row CHECK while the *transition-graph* rule is not).

**Soft Delete Strategy:** Standard — reserved for genuine data-entry correction (a cheque recorded in error); a real bounced/cancelled cheque is never soft-deleted, since it's a permanent operational and — for bounced cheques — potentially legal fact that must remain queryable for collections and dispute purposes.

**Audit Requirements:** All status transitions and date-field population events audit-logged; `bounced` transitions specifically are flagged `severity = 'critical'` in `audit_logs`, consistent with how `refresh_tokens` theft-detection events are elevated in Module 3 §3.6 — a bounced cheque is a comparably significant financial-risk event for this domain.

**Future Scalability Notes:** Scales with the fraction of `rent_payments` rows settled via cheque — per Jordanian market practice, potentially a large minority-to-plurality of all payments, hence the explicit 500,000+ row scale target. Partitioning analysis in §6.4 (shared with `rent_payments`).

## 6.3 Table: `payment_allocations`

**Purpose:** The explicit many-to-many linkage between a physical payment/receipt event (a `rent_payments` row acting in its "money received" capacity) and the specific billing obligation(s) that money settles (a `rent_payments` row acting in its "amount owed" capacity) — including the degenerate, overwhelmingly common 1:1 case, per the uniform-handling rationale in §6.0.

**Business Description:** Every settlement of rent, without exception, is recorded here. This is the table that makes partial payment, overpayment, multi-period settlement, and cheque-bounce reversal all representable without ever mutating a payment or obligation row's historical facts in place — the actual "how much of which obligation was settled by which specific receipt event, and when" ledger.

**Why a payment event and a billing obligation are both represented by rows in the same `rent_payments` table, linked via this junction, rather than two structurally distinct tables:** because in the dominant real-world case (a tenant pays exactly one month's rent, on time, in one transaction) the "obligation" and "the event that settles it" are conceptually the same fact from the business's point of view — Jordanian property managers do not think of "March rent owed" and "March rent received" as two different records when they coincide simply and immediately. Splitting them into two always-distinct tables would force the simple, dominant case through an unnecessary extra join and an extra row for the ~90%+ of payments that never need the flexibility, whereas the current design lets the simple case remain a single `rent_payments` row plus one trivial 1:1 `payment_allocations` row, while the complex cases (which are exactly where a second table earns its keep) use the same two tables in their fuller many-to-many capacity. This is a deliberate pragmatic middle ground, not an oversight — flagged explicitly here since a stricter textbook data-modeling instinct might prefer full separation, but that would add a mandatory join to the highest-volume, highest-frequency query in the entire schema for no benefit in the common case.

**Columns:**

| Column Name | PostgreSQL Data Type | Nullable | Default | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| id | UUID | NO | uuid_generate_v7() | Primary key |
| company_id | UUID | NO | — | Denormalized tenant scope |
| receiving_payment_id | UUID | NO | — | FK to `rent_payments(id)` — the payment event that physically received funds (the "source" of money in this allocation) |
| obligation_payment_id | UUID | NO | — | FK to `rent_payments(id)` — the billing obligation this allocation settles, in whole or in part (the "target" of money in this allocation) |
| allocated_amount | NUMERIC(12,3) | NO | — | The portion of `receiving_payment_id`'s total funds applied to `obligation_payment_id` — the core business-meaningful attribute of this M:M relationship, per the Phase 2 M:M-with-attributes principle |
| allocation_date | DATE | NO | CURRENT_DATE | The date this specific allocation was recorded/applied — may differ from the receiving payment's actual receipt date when reconciliation happens after the fact (e.g., an ambiguous bank transfer matched to a specific obligation days later) |
| allocation_status | allocation_status_enum | NO | 'active' | `active` \| `reversed` — see Business Rules; `reversed` is the mechanism for cheque-bounce and other settlement-reversal scenarios, never a hard delete |
| reversal_reason | VARCHAR(255) | YES | NULL | Required when `allocation_status = 'reversed'` — e.g. "cheque bounced," "duplicate allocation corrected," "tenant disputed and refunded" |
| reversed_at | TIMESTAMPTZ | YES | NULL | Required when `allocation_status = 'reversed'` |
| reversed_by | UUID | YES | NULL | FK to `users`; nullable for system-driven reversals (e.g., an automated bounce-processing job) |
| notes | TEXT | YES | NULL | — |
| created_at | TIMESTAMPTZ | NO | now() | — |
| updated_at | TIMESTAMPTZ | NO | now() | — |
| created_by | UUID | YES | NULL | — |
| updated_by | UUID | YES | NULL | — |
| deleted_at | TIMESTAMPTZ | YES | NULL | Soft delete — see Business Rules on the distinction between soft-delete (data-entry correction) and `allocation_status = 'reversed'` (real financial reversal) |
| deleted_by | UUID | YES | NULL | — |

**Primary Key:** `id`

**Foreign Keys:**
- `company_id` → `companies(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT.
- `receiving_payment_id` → `rent_payments(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT — per the stated "invalid payment allocations" integrity requirement, an allocation can never be orphaned from the payment event that funded it.
- `obligation_payment_id` → `rent_payments(id)` ON DELETE RESTRICT — same reasoning, for the settled-obligation side.
- `reversed_by` → `users(id)` ON DELETE SET NULL.
- `created_by`, `updated_by`, `deleted_by` → `users(id)` ON DELETE SET NULL.

**Unique Constraints:** None beyond the PK. Deliberately **no** uniqueness on `(receiving_payment_id, obligation_payment_id)` — a single receiving payment can legitimately create multiple allocation rows against the same obligation over time in rare correction scenarios (an initial allocation, a reversal, and a corrected re-allocation are three distinct rows, not one row mutated three times, per the append-only philosophy applied consistently to financial history throughout this schema) — blocking this at the constraint level would force an awkward update-in-place pattern that contradicts the module's own immutable-ledger design principle.

**Check Constraints:**
- `chk_payment_allocations_amount_positive` — `allocated_amount > 0` — an allocation of zero or negative amount is never meaningful; a reduction/correction is expressed by reversing the original allocation (`allocation_status = 'reversed'`) and, if needed, creating a new smaller one, never by an allocation row carrying a non-positive amount.
- `chk_payment_allocations_not_self_referencing` — `receiving_payment_id != obligation_payment_id` — a payment cannot allocate funds to itself; this would be a logical impossibility (money received *for* an obligation cannot simultaneously *be* the settlement event for that same obligation as a distinct fact) and is almost certainly indicative of an application bug if it ever occurred.
- `chk_payment_allocations_reversal_fields` — `(allocation_status = 'active' AND reversal_reason IS NULL AND reversed_at IS NULL) OR (allocation_status = 'reversed' AND reversal_reason IS NOT NULL AND reversed_at IS NOT NULL)` — the structural implementation ensuring a reversed allocation always carries its justification and timestamp, and an active allocation never carries stale reversal data from some earlier state.

**Over-allocation prevention (the stated "Over-allocation" and "Negative balances" integrity requirements, addressed in full):**
Over-allocation — allocating more money to an obligation than that obligation actually owes, or allocating more money out of a receiving payment than it actually received — **cannot** be prevented by a single-row CHECK constraint, since it is inherently an aggregate-across-rows question (PostgreSQL CHECK constraints cannot reference other rows, the identical limitation already encountered and solved for circular lease-renewal-chain prevention in Module 5 §5.1). The chosen mechanism, consistent with that precedent:
- **Database-level backstop via trigger:** a `BEFORE INSERT OR UPDATE ON payment_allocations FOR EACH ROW` trigger computes, within the same transaction, `SUM(allocated_amount) WHERE obligation_payment_id = NEW.obligation_payment_id AND allocation_status = 'active' AND deleted_at IS NULL` (including the new/updated row) and raises an exception if that sum would exceed `obligation_payment_id`'s own `rent_payments.amount_due` **unless** the obligation row's `payment_purpose = 'adjustment'` (an intentional credit/overpayment-absorbing row is explicitly allowed to exceed its own nominal `amount_due`, since that's precisely what an overpayment-handling adjustment row is for) — this is the direct enforcement of "prevent over-allocation" at the database level, not merely application-layer discipline, because allocation correctness is exactly the kind of financial-integrity invariant this schema treats as too important to leave to application code alone (the same posture as the partial-unique-index enforcement of "one active lease per apartment").
- **Symmetric check on the receiving side:** the same trigger also validates `SUM(allocated_amount) WHERE receiving_payment_id = NEW.receiving_payment_id AND allocation_status = 'active'` does not exceed the receiving payment's own recorded `amount_due`/actual-funds-received figure (for a receiving-side row, this is the amount that physically arrived) — preventing a payment event from being allocated out to *more* money than it actually contained, which would otherwise silently manufacture funds that don't exist.
- **Why a trigger and not solely application-layer validation:** identical justification to the circular-renewal-chain defense in Module 5 §5.1 — application-layer validation is the primary, cheap, first line of defense in the common case, but a direct SQL write (an admin data-fix script, a future service bypassing the application's business logic layer) must not be able to silently produce a financially-impossible state; the trigger is the non-negotiable backstop specifically because over-allocation is a correctness class of bug with real financial/legal consequences (an owner's P&L becoming wrong, a tenant being told they've overpaid when the ledger cannot actually support that claim), not merely a UX nicety.
- **Negative balances:** directly prevented by the combination of `chk_payment_allocations_amount_positive` (no allocation row can itself be negative) and `chk_rent_payments_amount_paid_nonneg` (the cached aggregate on the obligation side can never be negative) — between the two, no code path (correct application logic, buggy application logic, or direct SQL) can drive either an individual allocation or an obligation's cumulative settled amount below zero.

**Indexes:**
- `idx_payment_allocations_obligation_payment_id` on `(obligation_payment_id)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — **the** query the `rent_payments.amount_paid` maintenance trigger itself runs on every write (`SUM(allocated_amount) WHERE obligation_payment_id = ...`), and also the "show me every payment event that contributed to settling this specific obligation" detail-page drill-down (a tenant disputing "I paid this already" — staff need to see the exact contributing payment(s)).
  - *Queries served:* the maintenance trigger's aggregate; the obligation-detail "settlement history" view.
  - *Expected improvement:* without this index, the trigger's own `SUM()` — which runs on *every single allocation write*, i.e., on every payment recorded platform-wide — would sequential-scan a table headed toward millions of rows on every write, which would be catastrophic for write throughput; with it, the aggregate is a tight, indexed range-scan bounded to one obligation's (typically 1–3) contributing allocations.
  - *Why this is the single most important index in this table:* it sits directly in the write-path hot loop of the module's core financial-correctness mechanism, not merely a read-side convenience — a missing index here would silently degrade every single payment-recording operation as history accumulates, unlike a missing read-side index which merely slows a dashboard.
- `idx_payment_allocations_receiving_payment_id` on `(receiving_payment_id)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — the symmetric query for the over-allocation trigger's receiving-side check, and the "what did this payment event get applied to" drill-down (e.g., a large bank transfer that was split across three months' obligations — staff viewing the transfer's detail page need to see where each portion went).
  - *Queries served:* the trigger's receiving-side `SUM()` validation; the payment-event "where did this money go" breakdown view.
  - *Expected improvement:* identical class of write-path-critical benefit as the obligation-side index above.
- `idx_payment_allocations_company_allocation_date` composite on `(company_id, allocation_date DESC)` WHERE `deleted_at IS NULL` — supports company-wide "recent settlement activity" dashboard feeds and reconciliation reports (e.g., "everything allocated today," a daily close-out reconciliation screen).
- `idx_payment_allocations_status` composite on `(company_id, allocation_status)` WHERE `allocation_status = 'reversed' AND deleted_at IS NULL` — a deliberately narrow partial index supporting the "review all reversed allocations" audit/reconciliation screen (e.g., a monthly review of every bounce-driven or correction-driven reversal, a real financial-controls workflow) — partial specifically on `reversed` because active allocations (the overwhelming majority at any given time) are irrelevant to this specific audit query and would be pure dead weight in this index.

**Business Rules:**
- Every allocation row is immutable once created except for the `allocation_status`/`reversal_reason`/`reversed_at`/`reversed_by` fields, which transition exactly once from `active` to `reversed` and never back — a "re-activated" reversal is represented by a brand-new allocation row, never by flipping `reversed` back to `active`, preserving a clean, monotonic audit trail consistent with the append-only philosophy already established for `contract_status_history` (Module 5 §5.3) and `meter_readings` (Module 4 §4.8).
- A reversal (e.g., triggered by a cheque bounce per §6.2) is always an application-orchestrated transaction that (1) sets the allocation's `allocation_status = 'reversed'` with its justification fields, which (2) causes the `rent_payments.amount_paid`/`due_date_status` maintenance trigger to recompute both the `obligation_payment_id` row (its settled amount decreases) and implicitly reflects the `receiving_payment_id` row's own now-reduced "successfully applied" total — a single logical financial event correctly propagating through both linked obligation and receiving rows via the same trigger mechanism that handles ordinary allocation creation, with no special-cased reversal logic needed in the trigger itself (a reversed row is simply excluded from the `WHERE allocation_status = 'active'` aggregate, exactly as if it had never existed for calculation purposes, while remaining permanently visible for audit purposes).
- Soft-delete (`deleted_at`) on this table is reserved strictly for genuine data-entry mistakes (an allocation created against the wrong obligation by a fat-fingered staff selection, corrected within the same session before any downstream consequence like a receipt was generated) — distinct from `allocation_status = 'reversed'`, which represents a real financial event (money that was genuinely applied and later genuinely un-applied) that must remain fully visible, never soft-deleted, for audit and dispute-resolution purposes. This distinction — soft-delete for "this row should never have existed" vs. status-transition for "this row correctly recorded something that later changed" — mirrors the identical distinction already drawn for `lease_contracts` soft-delete-is-rare-vs.-status-driven-lifecycle (Module 5 §5.1).

**Soft Delete Strategy:** See Business Rules above — present per the Global Convention but used narrowly and rarely, with `allocation_status = 'reversed'` handling the actual business-lifecycle "undo" case instead.

**Audit Requirements:** Every allocation creation and every reversal is audit-logged; reversals are flagged high-severity given their direct effect on multiple financial records' settlement state via the maintenance trigger.

**Future Scalability Notes:** Scales as a small multiple of `rent_payments` (typically 1 allocation per payment in the dominant simple case, occasionally 2–3 for split/multi-period settlements) — at 2,000,000+ `rent_payments` rows, this table lands in a comparable low-millions range. Partitioning analysis in §6.4.

## 6.4 Scalability at Target Scale

| Scale point | Impact |
|---|---|
| 10,000 companies | Trivial for every table in this module in isolation — per-company row counts stay in the low hundreds to low thousands even at full portfolio size, well within any single index's efficient range. |
| 2,000,000 rent_payments | **This is the module's central scalability decision.** Unlike `lease_contracts` (Module 5 §5.7) and `apartments` (Module 4 §4.4) — both deliberately evaluated *against* partitioning and found not to need it because their query patterns aren't time-range-bounded — `rent_payments` **does** meet the partitioning test the other two fail: it is append-only-dominant (a billing-job-generated row, once settled, is rarely touched again except by the rare correction/reversal flow), naturally time-ordered via `due_date`, and every stated query shape (payment history, outstanding balances, late payments, dashboards) is bounded to a specific contract/tenant/company scope *and* frequently further bounded to a recent or bounded date range (dashboards ask about "this month," "the last 90 days," "currently overdue" — not "all payments ever" as an undifferentiated scan). **Recommendation: RANGE partition `rent_payments` by `due_date`, quarterly** (not monthly — see rationale below), **with an explicit `DEFAULT` partition** to hold every row whose `due_date IS NULL` (see below). |
| 500,000 cheque_details | Scales as a meaningful fraction of `rent_payments`. Given its lower absolute row count relative to `rent_payments` and — critically — its query patterns being dominated by *company/contract/tenant-scoped* lookups (§6.2's indexes) rather than *platform-wide time-range* scans, **partitioning is not recommended for `cheque_details`** at this target scale; the composite indexes already specified keep every stated query fast without the added operational complexity of partition management for a table an order of magnitude smaller than its partitioned sibling. |
| 2,000,000+ payment_allocations | Scales in lockstep with `rent_payments`. However, unlike `rent_payments`, this table's dominant query pattern (§6.3) is **not** time-range-scoped at all — it's overwhelmingly `obligation_payment_id`/`receiving_payment_id` equality lookups driven by the maintenance trigger and detail-page drill-downs, neither of which benefits from date-range partition pruning the way a dashboard's "overdue this month" query does. **Partitioning is not recommended for `payment_allocations`** for the same query-pattern-driven reason `apartments` and `lease_contracts` were excluded — row count alone doesn't justify it; the FK-equality-lookup indexes already specified are the correct scaling mechanism here, and partitioning would in fact actively *hurt* this table's dominant access pattern by potentially spreading a single obligation's few allocation rows across partition boundaries with no compensating benefit, since those rows aren't naturally date-clustered together the way a single meter's readings are. |

**Why quarterly, not monthly, partitioning for `rent_payments` — a deliberate deviation from the monthly convention used for `meter_readings`/`audit_logs`/`login_history`:** those three tables accumulate at a volume (100M+, 5M+, 20M+ respectively) and write-frequency (every meter read, every state change, every login attempt) that genuinely demands monthly granularity to keep individual partitions manageably small. `rent_payments` at 2,000,000 rows across (conservatively) a multi-year platform history writes far less densely per unit time — roughly (500,000 active contracts × 12 installments/year) at full target scale, meaning each **monthly** partition would hold on the order of 500,000 rows, which is already a very reasonable single-partition size, while a **quarterly** partition would hold on the order of 1.5 million — still comfortably manageable and, critically, reduces the total partition count by a factor of three over the multi-year retention horizon this table needs (unlike the security/logging tables, `rent_payments` cannot ever be bulk-dropped past a retention window — it's permanent financial/legal history, per Phase 1 §1.16 and Jordanian statutory retention requirements — so partition *count* accumulates forever and its own management overhead, not just per-partition size, is a real long-term consideration). Quarterly partitioning is the better-calibrated choice specifically because this table's growth rate is an order of magnitude lower than the true high-frequency event-log tables, and its partitions, unlike theirs, are never dropped — only ever added to.

**Partition key column and the `NULL due_date` case — resolved via an explicit `DEFAULT` partition, not by forcing `due_date` to be `NOT NULL`.** `due_date` is a genuine business fact (a real obligation's due date), and — per §6.0/§6.1 — is legitimately `NULL` for `unallocated_receipt` rows and for the majority of `adjustment` rows. Forcing `due_date` to be `NOT NULL` schema-wide, or backfilling it with a synthetic value such as `created_at::date` purely to give PostgreSQL's native RANGE-partitioning mechanism a non-NULL routing key, was considered and **rejected** — either approach would misrepresent an unreconciled receipt or a dateless adjustment as having a real contractual due date it does not have, corrupting the column's business meaning for an infrastructure concern.

The correct, chosen resolution: the partitioned `rent_payments` table includes an explicit `DEFAULT` partition (`CREATE TABLE rent_payments_default PARTITION OF rent_payments DEFAULT`), which is standard, fully-supported PostgreSQL RANGE-partitioning behavior. Every row with `due_date IS NULL` — i.e., every `unallocated_receipt` row and every `adjustment` row without a real due date — routes to this `DEFAULT` partition automatically; every row with a populated `due_date` (every `scheduled_installment` row, and any `adjustment` row that genuinely has one) routes to its normal quarterly partition as usual. This preserves `due_date`'s correct nullability and business meaning unchanged, requires no application-layer convention to be relied upon for partition-routing correctness (the `DEFAULT` partition is a database-level guarantee, not a convention that a stray code path could violate), and needs no schema addition beyond the partitioning migration itself.

The `DEFAULT` partition's size should be monitored at implementation/operations time: unreconciled receipts and dateless adjustments are expected to be a small, bounded fraction of total `rent_payments` volume relative to `scheduled_installment` rows, so it should not undermine the partitioning scheme's per-partition-size goals in practice. If real production data shows the `DEFAULT` partition growing disproportionately large, the partitioning strategy for that specific subset may be revisited at that time based on actual observed volume and query patterns — this is a forward-looking operational note, not a currently-known problem.

No other aspect of the partitioning design — grain (quarterly), key column choice (`due_date`), or the decision to partition `rent_payments` at all — changes as a result of this treatment.

**Should `cheque_details`/`payment_allocations` be revisited if `rent_payments` is partitioned?** No structural change is required to either child table — a partitioned parent's child tables with a simple FK to the parent's `id` continue to work identically in PostgreSQL regardless of whether the parent is partitioned (the FK target is the logical table, not a specific partition), so this remains a `rent_payments`-only physical change with zero migration impact on `cheque_details` or `payment_allocations`.

## 6.5 Database Integrity Summary

| Risk | Prevention Mechanism | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Duplicate receipts | `receipt_number` denormalized from `rent_payment_receipts` (Module 7), whose own `rent_payment_id UNIQUE NOT NULL` constraint (Phase 1 §1.20) is the true structural enforcement — "at most one receipt per rent payment" is guaranteed at the receipts-table level, not re-enforced redundantly here | This module's `rent_payments.receipt_number` is a read-convenience denormalization, not a second source of truth; the actual uniqueness guarantee lives where the receipt row itself is created |
| Duplicate cheque numbers within the same company | `uq_cheque_details_company_cheque_number` on `(company_id, cheque_number, bank_name)` | Bank-qualified to avoid false-positive rejection of coincidentally-matching numbers at different banks, while catching genuine same-bank duplicate entry |
| Invalid payment amounts | `chk_rent_payments_amount_due_nonneg`, `chk_rent_payments_amount_paid_nonneg`, `chk_payment_allocations_amount_positive`, `chk_cheque_details_amount_positive` | Every monetary column in this module has an explicit sign constraint matched to its actual valid domain (some allow zero, none allow inappropriate negatives) |
| Invalid cheque dates | `chk_cheque_details_due_after_issue`, `chk_cheque_details_received_after_issue`, `chk_cheque_details_deposit_requires_received` + `_deposit_after_received`, `chk_cheque_details_clearance_requires_deposit` + `_clearance_after_deposit`, `chk_cheque_details_bounce_requires_deposit`, `chk_cheque_details_status_date_consistency` | A comprehensive, layered set of CHECK constraints enforcing both individual date ordering and status/date-field correlation — no lifecycle-inconsistent cheque row is representable |
| Invalid payment allocations (orphaned) | `payment_allocations.receiving_payment_id`/`obligation_payment_id` both `NOT NULL` with real FKs, `ON DELETE RESTRICT` | No allocation can exist without valid, permanent references to both sides of the settlement |
| Invalid payment allocations (self-referencing) | `chk_payment_allocations_not_self_referencing` | A payment cannot be recorded as settling itself |
| Over-allocation (obligation side) | `BEFORE INSERT OR UPDATE` trigger validating `SUM(active allocations) <= amount_due` (adjustment-purpose rows exempted) | Aggregate-across-rows validation, which a CHECK constraint alone cannot express — the same class of problem already solved via trigger/uniqueness-backstop for circular lease-renewal chains (Module 5) |
| Over-allocation (receiving side) | Same trigger, symmetric validation against the receiving payment's actual received funds | Prevents allocating out more money than a payment event actually contained |
| Negative balances | `chk_rent_payments_amount_paid_nonneg` + `chk_payment_allocations_amount_positive` in combination | No code path, correct or buggy, can drive a cached settlement total or an individual allocation below zero |
| Duplicate billing-period obligations for the same contract | `uq_rent_payments_contract_period` on `(lease_contract_id, billing_period_start, billing_period_end)`, scoped to `scheduled_installment` rows | Prevents the billing job (or manual entry) from double-generating the same period's obligation |
| Reversal without justification | `chk_payment_allocations_reversal_fields` | A reversed allocation always carries a reason and timestamp; an active one never carries stale reversal data |
| Bounced cheque with no reason on record | `chk_cheque_details_status_date_consistency` (the `bounced` branch requires both `bounce_date` and `bounce_reason`) | Mirrors the `chk_contract_terminations_deduction_reason` dispute-prevention pattern from Module 5 |
| Scheduled installment missing period/due-date data | `chk_rent_payments_scheduled_period_purpose` | A `scheduled_installment` row must always carry `billing_period_start`, `billing_period_end`, and `due_date`; non-scheduled rows must not carry period dates |
| Late/overdue status derived without a real due date | Trigger logic (§6.1) explicitly excludes `late`/`overdue_unpaid` from the derivation for any row with `due_date IS NULL` | A row with no due date cannot be "late" or "overdue" relative to a date that does not exist for it |
| Partition-routing failure for `NULL`-`due_date` rows | Explicit PostgreSQL `DEFAULT` partition on the partitioned `rent_payments` table (§6.4) | Rows with `due_date IS NULL` route deterministically to the `DEFAULT` partition — a database-level guarantee, not an application-layer convention |

## 6.6 Performance Optimization Explanation

- **Payment History (contract-scoped):** `idx_rent_payments_contract_due_date` — equality-leading composite with `DESC` trailing sort column, turning the module's single most common query into a direct ordered index scan.
- **Tenant Payment History:** `idx_rent_payments_tenant_due_date` — identical pattern, rooted at the tenant rather than the contract, made possible only because `tenant_id` is denormalized onto `rent_payments` per §6.0 rather than requiring a join through `lease_contracts` on this module's highest-volume table.
- **Contract Payment History:** covered by the same `idx_rent_payments_contract_due_date` index as "Payment History" above — the stated requirements list these as if potentially distinct, but they resolve to the identical query shape and are correctly served by one index, not two redundant ones.
- **Outstanding Balances:** `idx_rent_payments_company_status_due_date` and the narrower `idx_rent_payments_late_overdue` partial index — both structured so a company-wide "what's owed right now" query is a targeted range-scan, never a sequential scan, regardless of how large the table's full historical row count grows.
- **Late Payments:** the same `idx_rent_payments_late_overdue` partial index, deliberately kept small by its `WHERE due_date_status IN ('late','overdue_unpaid')` scope so it stays fast indefinitely even as settled/historical rows accumulate elsewhere in the table.
- **Dashboard Statistics:** `idx_rent_payments_company_status_due_date` (rent side) and `idx_cheque_details_company_status_due_date` (cheque side) together cover every stated dashboard tile shape (collections aging, overdue totals, upcoming cheque deposits, bounced-cheque follow-up counts) without needing a bespoke index per tile.
- **Search:** `idx_rent_payments_receipt_number` (exact), `idx_rent_payments_payment_reference_trgm` (fuzzy bank-reference search), and `idx_cheque_details_cheque_number_trgm` (fuzzy cheque-number search) together cover every stated free-text/fragment search need across this module's two searchable identifier types.
- **Filtering:** every dashboard/worklist filtering shape named in the brief (by status, by date range, by company, by contract, by tenant) is covered by one of the composite indexes above — no filtering requirement in this module lacks a matching leading-column-correct index.
- **Pagination:** every composite index with a trailing `DESC` date column directly supports keyset pagination (`WHERE (lease_contract_id, due_date) < ($1, $2) ORDER BY due_date DESC LIMIT 50`), the same preferred-over-`OFFSET` strategy established in Module 3 §3.10, essential here given this module's tables are the fastest-growing in the schema after `meter_readings`.
- **Write-Path Performance (the module's unique concern relative to Modules 3–5):** unlike the mostly-read-optimized indexing concerns of prior modules, this module has a genuinely write-path-critical index pair (`idx_payment_allocations_obligation_payment_id`, `idx_payment_allocations_receiving_payment_id`) that sits inside the over-allocation-prevention trigger's own aggregate query, executed on *every* payment-recording write platform-wide — these two indexes were prioritized and verified first, ahead of every read-side convenience index, precisely because a missing index here would degrade the throughput of the module's core financial-correctness mechanism itself, not merely a dashboard's load time.

## Module 6 (Rent Payments & Cheque Handling) — Architecture Review

- **PostgreSQL Best Practices:** UUIDv7 PKs throughout; `TIMESTAMPTZ` for all point-in-time columns (`reversed_at`, audit columns) versus correctly-typed `DATE` for calendar-date concepts (`due_date`, `billing_period_start`/`end`, `issue_date`, `deposit_date`, etc.) — the identical DATE-vs-TIMESTAMPTZ discipline applied correctly and consistently since Module 5; `NUMERIC(12,3)` for every JOD monetary column paired with an explicit `currency` column, matching the Global Convention exactly across all three tables.
- **Security:** Every table carries `company_id` and is RLS-ready under the standard tenant-isolation policy; no non-standard RLS shape is needed anywhere in this module (unlike Module 3's `refresh_tokens`/`login_history`), since every row in every table here belongs unambiguously to one company. Bounced-cheque events and allocation reversals are elevated to `critical`/high-severity in `audit_logs`, consistent with how comparably financially-significant events are treated elsewhere (refresh-token theft in Module 3, deposit deductions in Module 5).
- **Financial Integrity:** This is the module where financial-integrity engineering is most concentrated in the entire schema. The layered defense on over-allocation (CHECK constraints for single-row invariants, a `BEFORE INSERT/UPDATE` trigger for the aggregate-across-rows invariant CHECK constraints cannot express) mirrors the identical layered-defense posture already validated for circular lease-renewal-chain prevention in Module 5 §5.1, applied here to a problem of comparable structural difficulty. The cached `amount_paid`/`due_date_status` columns on `rent_payments` are explicitly documented as derived, trigger-maintained, single-source-of-truth-is-elsewhere columns — never a silent 3NF violation — consistent with how every other cached column in this schema (`apartments.occupancy_status`, `utility_meters.last_reading_value`, `buildings.total_apartments_count`) has been treated since Module 4. The `due_date_status` derivation now explicitly consults `company_settings.rent_grace_period_days`, making a previously-captured-but-dormant configuration setting functionally live, and the enum's previously-undocumented `bounced` value has been removed in favor of the already-correct, non-duplicative `cheque_details.status = 'bounced'` representation.
- **Index Quality:** Every composite index in this module was checked for column-order correctness (equality-before-range/sort, `DESC` embedded where a query needs most-recent-first) — no index found with the wrong leading column. The write-path-critical `payment_allocations` indexes were identified and called out as categorically different in importance from the module's read-side convenience indexes, a distinction not explicitly drawn in prior modules (Modules 3–5's hottest indexes were all *read*-path; this module is the first where a *write*-path aggregate query is the dominant performance concern) — flagged here as a genuinely new consideration this module introduces rather than a repeated pattern.
- **Constraint Quality:** The cheque lifecycle's date-consistency rules (`chk_cheque_details_status_date_consistency` and its supporting per-transition CHECK constraints) were deliberately reviewed against the real-world "cheque bounces, then is re-deposited and clears" scenario and a rule was consciously **not** added (mutual exclusivity of `bounce_date` and `status = 'cleared'`) specifically because it would have rejected this legitimate, common banking practice — called out explicitly here as a constraint that was considered and correctly rejected, not merely omitted by oversight, continuing the "show the caught near-miss" transparency discipline established in Module 3 §3.5's `idx_role_permissions_role_id` redundancy callout. The `chk_rent_payments_scheduled_period_purpose` constraint was likewise reviewed to ensure `due_date`'s nullability continues to reflect its real business meaning (nullable for non-scheduled rows) rather than being forced non-NULL for an infrastructure/partitioning convenience — a deliberate, documented preservation of domain semantics over schema convenience.
- **Query Performance:** Every stated required query shape (Payment History, Tenant Payment History, Contract Payment History, Outstanding Balances, Late Payments, Dashboard Statistics, Search, Filtering, Pagination) is explicitly mapped to a specific index in §6.6, with no requirement left unaddressed and no index added without a corresponding named requirement — the same discipline as Module 4's four-named-query-shapes-to-indexes mapping for `apartments`.
- **Multi-Tenant Isolation:** Confirmed — every table denormalizes `company_id` directly; no table in this module needs a non-standard RLS shape.
- **Future Scalability:** `rent_payments` is correctly identified as this module's sole partitioning candidate — and, notably, the **first table in the entire schema where the standard monthly partitioning convention was deliberately overridden** (quarterly instead) with an explicit growth-rate-based justification, rather than mechanically reapplying the monthly pattern used for `meter_readings`/`audit_logs`/`login_history` without checking whether it actually fits this table's different write-density profile — this is the correct, non-mechanical application of the partitioning principle, consistent with how `apartments` and `lease_contracts` were correctly excluded from partitioning in Modules 4 and 5 despite their large row counts. The partitioning design now explicitly documents a `DEFAULT` partition to correctly and safely route rows whose `due_date IS NULL`, without distorting `due_date`'s business meaning or relying on an unenforced application-layer convention. `cheque_details` and `payment_allocations` were both deliberately evaluated *against* partitioning and found not to need it, with the reasoning made explicit (dominant query patterns are FK-equality-lookup-driven, not time-range-driven) rather than defaulting to "large table therefore partition."
- **Maintainability:** Every non-obvious modeling decision in this module (the payment-event/billing-obligation duality within one `rent_payments` table, the `payment_allocations` M:M-with-reversal design, the quarterly-not-monthly partitioning deviation, the deliberately-rejected bounce/cleared mutual-exclusivity constraint, the grace-period-aware `due_date_status` derivation, the removal of the undocumented `bounced` enum value, the restored/clarified `due_date` nullability rule, and the `DEFAULT`-partition resolution for `NULL`-`due_date` rows) is documented in place with its specific business and technical reasoning, continuing the "no undocumented deviation" discipline maintained without exception since Module 1.
